3. The money math (read this first, everything depends on it)

The case gives three numbers: a $5 cost for each customer given the free trial, a $15 irritation cost when a targeted customer does not buy, and $30 of value when a targeted customer does buy. Turning that into profit per targeted customer:

Targeted customer...CalculationProfit
buys$30 value - $5 trial+$25
does not buy-$5 trial - $15 irritation-$20

Break-even: contacting a customer with purchase probability p is worth it when 25p - 20(1-p) > 0, i.e. p > 20/45 = 0.444. Big Owl used 0.5, which is not the economic threshold.

Hand check you can do in 30 seconds. Neural network at 0.5 targets 281 customers, 154 buy, 127 do not.
Big Owl's formula: 30 x 154 - 5 x 281 = 4,620 - 1,405 = $3,215 (irritation ignored).
Correct formula: 25 x 154 - 20 x 127 = 3,850 - 2,540 = $1,310.
The gap is 127 x $15 = $1,905, exactly the missing irritation cost.

Assumption to confirm as a group: we treat the $30 as revenue, the $5 trial as a cost on every targeted customer, and the $15 as a cost only on targeted non-buyers. This follows the wording of the case.

How to check this step
  • Recompute one number by hand or in a pivot table: purchase rate for family size 4 should be about 9.8% (3,261 customers).
  • Note that Big Owl's own describe() output contains an index column (Unnamed: 0) and a leftover cluster_label column, so averages of those are meaningless. Ours selects only the six real columns.
  • Big Owl calls usage "daily"; the case says minutes per week.
Step 2 · Section 2

Clustering (customer segments)

What was done. K-means clustering on the five variables, in two versions: standardized (each variable rescaled to comparable size, our version) and unstandardized (Big Owl's version). K = 4 in both. For each cluster we show size, average age, family size, weekly minutes, share on 4K, purchase rate, and profit per targeted customer. Plus an elbow/silhouette plot, a radar plot scaled to the full data range, and two stability checks.

Why. Without standardizing, TV resolution (values 1080 or 2160) and weekly minutes (up to 250) dominate the distance calculation, and family size (2 to 4) barely counts. Big Owl also never looked at whether clusters buy differently.

1 Young 4K viewers2,92123100100%24%-$9.15
2 Young HD viewers2,977231010%33%-$5.07

Result. The segments split on age, usage and resolution only. Family size is about 3.0 and screens about 1.5 in every cluster. Buying rates only range from 24% to 33%, all below the 44.4% break-even, so targeting a whole segment loses money. Big Owl's unstandardized clusters range from 24% to 37%, also below break-even.

How to check this step
  • K = 4 is a convenience choice, not proven. The elbow curve is smooth and silhouette scores (0.22 to 0.33) keep rising up to K = 10. We used 4 only to compare with Big Owl.
  • Agreement between the two versions: adjusted Rand index 0.47 (1 = identical, 0 = random), so standardizing changes about half the assignments.
  • Stability across random starts: rerunning with seeds 2 to 5 gave 0.55, 1.0, 1.0, 1.0. One alternative start gives a noticeably different solution, so treat these segments as descriptive, not sacred. Big Owl does not set a seed at all, so its clusters change from run to run.
  • The radar plot scales each variable to the range of the full data. (Scaling only the four cluster means, as Big Owl did, exaggerates tiny differences.)
Step 3 · Section 3

Logistic regression (explanation only)

What was done. Three versions of a logistic regression: (A) Big Owl's exact specification, (B) family size as categories and 4K as a yes/no variable, (C) same as B but with age and usage standardized so effects are on a comparable scale. We report odds ratios (how much the odds of buying multiply), 95% confidence intervals and p-values.

Why. Big Owl compared raw coefficients measured in different units (per pixel, per year, per minute), which cannot be ranked against each other.

How to check this step
  • Our version of Big Owl's model reproduces its coefficients exactly (0.0098, -1.2617, 0.0010, -0.0006, -0.0291), so Big Owl's arithmetic is right. What is wrong is the interpretation: it says resolution has a "smaller impact", but the -0.0006 is per pixel, and the 1,080-pixel move from HD to 4K roughly halves the odds.
  • Family size as a category vs a single number barely changes fit (AIC 10,327.8 vs 10,328.8), so this is a minor point and should not be oversold in the report.
  • Pseudo R-squared is 0.159: these five variables explain only a modest share of buying behavior. Expect no model to be very accurate.
  • These are associations from one pilot, not proof of cause.

6. The recommendation and the open decisions

CFO objective: maximize profit. Use the average of the RF and NN scores and contact customers scoring 0.444 or higher. On the 1,000 test customers: 310 targeted, 169 buyers, $1,405 profit; contact everyone loses $5,780 and contact no one earns $0. Profit stays flat when the cutoff moves around, so the recommendation is not sensitive to the exact rule.
Decisions the group still needs to make
  1. Can a segment rule ("target family size 2") be the final recommendation? The task says "targeting model" and grades against the given RF and NN predictions, so a model-based headline is the safe choice, with the family size 2 rule ($1,665) shown as a benchmark. If the professor says a segment rule is fine, it becomes the headline. This question is worth sending to the professor.
  2. Is the payoff interpretation right? (+$25 and -$20 per targeted customer, see section 3.)
  3. How much do we say about K = 4? We suggest one honest sentence: it was chosen for comparability, not because the data supports it.

The 3-slide deck follows the same story: (1) Big Owl's $3,215 is really $1,310, (2) family size drives buying and segments miss it, (3) the CFO recommendation.

Tick these as you go (nothing is saved). If any fail, that is exactly what to raise with the group.

Known limitations (be upfront about these)

  • Everything is evaluated on one test set of 1,000 customers, so numbers move by a few hundred dollars from sampling noise (the bootstrap ranges show this).
  • Rules like "best N" are picked on the same rows they are scored on, so they are optimistic.
  • Family size 2 with 4K is only just above break-even.
  • Results are associations from one pilot. A small holdout group in the real rollout would confirm them.

8. Glossary

Break-even probability (0.444)
The purchase probability above which contacting a customer earns money on average: 20 / (25 + 20).
Precision, recall, F1
Precision: of those we targeted, the share who bought. Recall: of all buyers, the share we reached. F1 mixes the two but ignores dollars.
Threshold vs top-N
A threshold contacts everyone scoring above a cutoff. Top-N contacts the N highest scores.
Standardizing
Rescaling each variable to a comparable size so no variable dominates just because of its units.
Silhouette score, inertia (elbow)
Two ways to judge how many clusters fit. Here neither points to a clear answer.
Adjusted Rand index (ARI)
How similar two clusterings are (1 identical, 0 random).
Odds ratio
How much the odds of buying multiply for a change in a variable. Below 1 means lower odds, above 1 means higher.
Calibration
Whether predicted probabilities match reality (70% predicted should mean about 70% actually buy).
Bootstrap
Resampling the test customers many times to see how much a result would wobble by chance.
